01 Sensation & Perception

Sensation & Perception

Sensation

Definition: When a stimulus activates the receptors of a sense organ; awareness of changing conditions.

Stimulus

Something that causes a bodily response

Stimulus Receptor
Light Retina
Sound Hair cells
Pressure Skin

Sensory Thresholds

Absolute Threshold

  • Definition: Weakest amount of a stimulus required to produce a sensation
  • Key fact: Detected 50% of the time

Difference Threshold

  • Definition: Minimum amount of difference a person can detect between two stimuli
  • Example: Optometrist testing your vision

Weber's Law

The larger/stronger a stimulus, the greater the change needed to notice any difference.

Sensory Adaptation

Definition: Get used to the level of a sensation and respond only to changes away from it

Purpose of Sensory Adaptation

  • Survival
  • Prevent overstimulation

Example: Vision adapts in darker areas

Signal-Detection Theory

  • Definition: Tendency to detect the presence of stimuli correctly due to motivation, skill, and competing stimuli
  • Key argument: Argues against a single threshold

Types of Processing

  • Pre-attentive (automatic): Unconscious processing
  • Attentive (careful attention): Conscious, deliberate focus

Perception

Definition: The way we interpret sensations and organize them into meaningful experiences.

Examples:

  • Seeing a car
  • Hearing a song on the radio
  • Feeling a desk

Learning to Perceive

Perception is shaped by more than the raw sensory input we receive. It is influenced by:

  • Needs: When we want something, we are more likely to perceive it
  • Beliefs and expectations: We tend to notice things that fit what we already believe
  • Previous experience: Past experience helps us interpret new sensory information

These factors help construct the brain's perception tools, which allow us to make sense of the world quickly and efficiently.

Perception Tools

Gestalt Principles

The brain automatically tries to organize separate pieces of information into wholes.

Five Gestalt Principles:

  • Proximity: Things that are close together are seen as belonging together
  • Continuity: The brain prefers smooth, continuous patterns
  • Similarity: Similar things are grouped together
  • Simplicity: The brain favors the simplest interpretation possible
  • Closure: The brain fills in missing information to complete a whole

Figure-Ground Perception

The ability to distinguish between a figure and its background.

Examples in senses other than sight:

  • Walking into a bakery and smelling cinnamon while filtering out every other smell
  • Eating a big meal and being able to taste the lemon inside of it

Visual Perception Terms

  • Retinal disparity: The difference between the images each eye receives; a binocular cue that helps the brain compute depth (greater disparity = closer object).
  • Convergence: Eyes turn inward (converge) for near objects; feedback from the eye muscles provides a cue to depth and distance.
  • Constancy: Perceive objects as stable and unchanging despite changes in viewing angle, distance, or lighting. Common forms include size constancy, shape constancy, and brightness (color) constancy.
  • Illusions: Perceptions that incorrectly represent physical stimuli; visual illusions reveal how perceptual systems use cues and assumptions, sometimes producing systematic errors.
